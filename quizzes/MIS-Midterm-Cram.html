Information Systems in Global Business Today

What an information system does

Environmentcustomers · suppliers · regulators · stockholders · competitors

UPS example: label scan (input) → central computer updates location (processing) → tracking page and proof of delivery (output).

Data → Information → Knowledge

Dataraw facts: "item 3456 sold, Tk 120"
Informationmeaningful: "rice sales up 20% this week"
Knowledgehow to act: "stock more rice before Eid"

IT = hardware and software. IS = IT plus the people, organization and management that use it. IS is the bigger idea.

Three dimensions of an IS

Organizationpeople, structure, business processes, culture, politics
Managementsets strategy, makes decisions, solves problems, leads
Technologyhardware, software, data management, networking = IT infrastructure

Six strategic business objectives (with the UPS case)

1 · Operational excellenceefficiency, lower cost. ORION routes saved ~100M miles.
2 · New products, services, business modelsonline labels, live tracking, DeliveryDefense.
3 · Customer & supplier intimacycustomers track and reroute parcels.
4 · Improved decision makingreal data instead of guesses. NPT reroutes around storms.
5 · Competitive advantagebetter service at lower cost than FedEx.
6 · SurvivalIS is a necessity (industry change, laws). Citibank ATMs.

Complementary assets

IT alone does not create value. Firms also need:

  • Organizational: supportive culture, efficient processes, teamwork
  • Managerial: strong senior support, incentives, training
  • Social: internet and telecom infrastructure, standards, laws

Digital firm + today's big four (1.7)

  • Digital firm: core business relationships and processes are digital. Time shifting (24/7) and space shifting (work anywhere).
  • IoT: everyday objects with sensors that share data
  • Big data: the 3 Vs (volume, variety, velocity)
  • Cloud computing: computing over the internet
  • AI: systems that learn and decide like people
Quick check: "Information technology and information systems are the same thing." True or false?

False. IT is the hardware and software. An IS also includes the organization and management dimensions.

Quick check: A manager runs "what if fuel rises 10%?" on shipping costs. Which system?

DSS. Non-routine, what-if analysis for middle management. (An MIS would just print the routine monthly cost report.)

Case 2 in one line: how do virtual meetings help and hurt?

Help: remote/hybrid work, meet from anywhere. Hurt: too many meetings, Zoom fatigue, isolation, surveillance, self-censorship. Fix: fewer, shorter meetings and meeting-free days (3 days: +55% cooperation, −57% stress).

CHAPTER 3 · 13 MIN · MOST MARKS

Information Systems, Organizations and Strategy

Porter's competitive forces model

Threat of new entrantsbarriers: cost of entry, know-how, economies of scale, brand, technology
Supplier powerfew suppliers, unique input, high switching cost → strong
Rivalry among existing competitorsmany rivals, same product, low switching cost, low loyalty, high exit cost → strong
Customer (buyer) powereasy to switch, price-sensitive, big buyers, price comparison online → strong
Threat of substitutescheaper or better alternatives that do the same job
How to answer: name the force → say strong / medium / low → give one fact as the reason. Example: "Buyer power is strong because airline fares are compared online and switching costs nothing."

Four strategies to deal with the forces

Low-cost leadershiplowest operating cost and price. Walmart continuous replenishment: sales data goes straight to suppliers.
Product differentiationnew, unique products or services. Apple iPhone/iPad, Nike custom shoes.
Focus on market nicheserve one narrow segment better than anyone. Hilton OnQ guest data.
Customer & supplier intimacystrong ties, high switching costs. Amazon recommendations, Toyota with suppliers.

How the internet changed each force

ForceInternet impact

Suppliersonline procurement → firm gains power over suppliers, but suppliers gain from lower entry barriers too

Two views of an organization

Technical (microeconomic)inputs (capital, labor) → production → outputs
Behavioralrights, privileges, routines, politics, culture
  • Routines: standard procedures; a business process is a set of routines
  • Mintzberg structures: entrepreneurial · machine bureaucracy · divisionalized bureaucracy · professional bureaucracy · adhocracy

Economic impacts of IT

  • Transaction cost theory: IT cuts the cost of dealing with outside firms → firms shrink / outsource
  • Agency theory: IT cuts the cost of supervising agents → firm can grow without more managers
  • IT flattens organizations: fewer middle-manager layers
  • Resistance to change (Leavitt's diamond): technology, tasks, people, structure all affect each other
  • Disruptive technology: replaces a product and creates winners and losers (PCs, digital photography, streaming music)

Network-based strategies

  • Synergies: output of one unit is input for another
  • Core competency: what the firm does best (world-class)
  • Network economics: value grows as more people join
  • Virtual company: links partners without owning them
  • Business ecosystem: keystone firm (platform owner like Apple) + niche firms (app makers)

Challenges with strategic systems

  • Advantage doesn't last: rivals copy
  • Align IT with business objectives
  • Managing strategic transitions: changing the whole organization is hard

AI in Ch 3: AI adds value at every value-chain step and can change each force (e.g. AI chatbots as new substitutes, AI pricing raises rivalry).

Apply it: airline industry, rate the five forces

Rivalry strong (same routes, same prices) · new entrants low to medium (huge cost, regulation) · suppliers strong (only Boeing and Airbus, fuel, unions) · buyers strong (compare fares online) · substitutes medium (bus/train for short trips only).

Apply it: which strategy is "Amazon remembers what you bought and recommends more"?

Customer intimacy. It builds a relationship and raises switching costs.

Databases, Information Management and Blockchain

From the textbook (no class slides yet): check with your notes.

Problems with the old file approach

  • Data redundancy (same data in many files) and inconsistency
  • Program-data dependence: change data, break programs
  • Lack of flexibility for new reports
  • Poor security
  • No data sharing

Fix: a DBMS (database management system) holds data centrally for many programs. It separates the logical view (what users see) from the physical view (how it's stored).

Table = relation · row = record (tuple) · column = field (attribute). Primary key identifies each row. Foreign key links to another table's primary key.

3 operations

  • Select: pick rows
  • Project: pick columns
  • Join: combine tables

DBMS capabilities

  • Data definition
  • Data dictionary: describes each field
  • Query language: SQL

Good design

  • Normalization: remove redundancy
  • ER diagram: entities and relationships
  • Referential integrity: no orphan foreign keys
  • NoSQL for unstructured data

Data mining finds: associations (bought together) · sequences (in order over time) · classification · clustering · forecasting. A data mart is a small warehouse for one department. A data lake stores raw data of any kind.

  • Distributed ledger: every participant has a copy; no central owner

Data quality words: information policy (rules for data) · data administration (policy and planning) · database administration (technical design and upkeep) · data governance · data quality audit · data cleansing / scrubbing (fix dirty data).
